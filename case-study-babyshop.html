Rebuilding the basket and product page for Babyshop

Babyshop was getting a new brand identity, and its product page and basket had to grow into it. A new look was not enough, so I audited the experience and designed features that help parents find what they need and buy with confidence. The product page comes first. The basket is coming soon.

Where it started

Babyshop is one of Landmark Group’s retail brands in the Gulf region, and its shoppers are parents, buying on the app and the website. For a very long time it had run on the same branding.

Then the whole system began moving from a legacy platform to a new one. It was the right moment for a change, and Babyshop was given a fresh look and a new brand identity. The product page had to grow into it.

But a new look was not going to be enough. If the page was being rebuilt anyway, it was the moment to ask better questions: what do parents need to know before they buy, and what could the page do for them, and for sales, that it did not do yet?

The problem

The old page was rigid and had no real structure. Its layout did not reflect the brand or the products Babyshop sells, and it no longer stood up well against the market. A lot of the information was either hidden or far too long. Getting from choosing a product to adding it to the basket and buying it took more effort than it should. And the perks that give a parent a reason to buy were there, but they were not upfront.

What I wanted

A more premium experience, built around better product decisions.

The goal was not only to make the page look better under Babyshop’s new brand. I wanted it to feel more considered, more structured and more useful, so parents could find what they need and buy with confidence. That meant bringing the new brand language in, and rethinking how product information and supporting features were organised across the app and the website.

I set four objectives.

  • 01

    Bring the new brand to life

    A cleaner, more cohesive, premium-feeling page that reflects Babyshop’s refreshed identity without compromising usability.

  • 02

    Make product information work harder

    Materials, specifications and care instructions that are easier to find and understand.

  • 03

    Adapt the page to the product category

    One consistent foundation, with relevant features for clothing and accessories, toys, mother-care products and furniture.

  • 04

    Support decisions beyond the product itself

    Practical help such as installation preferences, material details and self-guides for assembly and care, so customers know what to expect before and after they buy.

What this meant for the page

The page had to feel cohesive but stay flexible enough for different needs. The core product information and the purchase journey would stay consistent across categories, while extra details and features would adapt to what was being viewed.

For clothing, that could mean clearer fabric composition and care information. For toys, accessible material details. For furniture, installation options and assembly guidance.

One cohesive shopping experience, with the right information and features for each product category.

How this connects to the design decisions

  • Brand consistency

    A refined visual hierarchy, clearer spacing and typography, and alignment with the new brand guidelines.

  • Better information architecture

    Essential product details first, with supporting information organised for easy discovery.

  • Category-specific features

    Material details, installation preferences and self-guides, where they are relevant to the product.

  • A cohesive app and web experience

    A consistent brand and interaction language, adapted to each platform’s layout and behaviour.

How I solved it

I started by looking, not drawing. First an audit of the existing app screens. Then a look at how others do it: a few baby brands in Dubai, a French brand, and IKEA and Walmart for the furniture side. From there I whiteboarded the gaps, and designed one foundation for the page, with the right extras for each category.

The hardest call: shipping in two chunks

We had very limited time, so shipping the whole product in one go was not possible. We split it into two chunks. First came the full revamp of the product page. Second came the new features: material details, care and installation, which arrive in MVP phases.

What I tried, and what I gave up

What I tried, and it shipped

  • Old product names were long, running titles that made little sense
  • Separated the brand from the product name, so each stands on its own
  • Made the brand name clickable, opening a brand listing page inside Babyshop
  • Parents can now buy directly from that brand's page too

What I gave up

  • Not every decision made it
  • Wanted subtle icons on the listing page, carried through to the product page, for material and care
  • Dropped: not feasible, and did not line up with the rest of the system
  • Wanted parents to choose an installation time and a delivery date
  • Settled for a simple yes or no on installation, plus the cost

My part in it

Owning the product page, from audit to prototype.

I was the only designer on this. While Babyshop’s wider brand refresh was underway, I took the new direction and turned it into a more useful product page, across the app and the website. I worked closely with my Product Manager, Babyshop’s Brand Manager and the web and app engineering teams, who built what I designed.

What I owned

  • Experience audit: reviewed the existing app screens to find opportunities on the product page.
  • Competitive analysis: studied how others present product information, care and installation: a few baby brands in Dubai, a French brand, and IKEA and Walmart for furniture.
  • Problem definition and ideation: used the research and whiteboarding to find the gaps and explore solutions.
  • Feature definition: defined and specified the features each product category needs.
  • UX and UI design: designed the information structure, the visual hierarchy and the interactions, across web and app.
  • Prototyping: prototyped the interactions, then worked with engineers as they built them.

Who I worked with

  • Product Manager: product priorities, feature requirements and the intended customer experience.
  • Brand Manager, Babyshop: making sure the page reflected the refreshed brand.
  • Web and app engineering teams: feasibility and building the designs. They are still building some of the features.

The brand refresh gave the visual direction. My part was to turn that direction into a page that is more useful: consistent with the brand, clearer about the product, and relevant to what each parent is buying.

Closing the gap

Engineers are still building parts of this. This is the path from design to live, and how I stayed close to it.

  1. Refine first. Before anything went to development, the design went through a few rounds of refinement. I talked it through with my manager and showed it to my fellow designers, asked what could work and what could not, and refined it from their feedback.
  2. Hand it over. I annotated the designs and left comments, and prototyped the interactions, so engineering could pick them up.
  3. Build alongside. I worked very closely with the engineering team as they built it.
  4. Test with QA. A separate QA team then tested the build, checking whether each feature was live and whether every use case worked.
  5. My own design QA. I did a pass of my own on padding, spacing and elements, and on brand switching. Babyshop sits on a layer of brand tokens, so I checked that the tokens behaved, and whether the component tokens, which make switching between brands simpler, were coming through.

What changed

I do not have launch numbers yet, so I will not quote any. Here is what I can say today.

  • The business has been doing well since the revamp, and the brands were happy with it, as were the CEO and customers.
  • Brand pages and material details have shipped.
  • Installation and self-guides are in development for the MVP.

Looking back

A few opportunities stood out during the design process that I would have loved to take further.

  • Brand consistency: The age-based colour system was designed for marketing and in-store use, but wasn't approved for digital UI. I would have aligned with the brand team earlier to explore how it could improve age-group recognition online.
  • Installation scheduling: Installation was handled outside the digital shopping journey, with customers contacted after purchase. I would have explored enabling customers to select a preferred installation date and time during checkout, making the process more seamless and predictable.
  • Offer visibility: While working on the basket experience, I identified an opportunity to surface cart-value offers, free gifts, and product-linked promotions earlier on the PLP. Showing these incentives during product discovery could encourage engagement and help customers make more informed purchase decisions.

Key learning: Some of the biggest opportunities lie beyond individual screens. Connecting brand, service, and promotional experiences across the customer journey can make the overall shopping experience more cohesive and conversion-focused.
