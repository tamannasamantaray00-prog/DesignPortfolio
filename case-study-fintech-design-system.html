Context

Before any screen got drawn, there was a more basic question sitting underneath this whole project: do you design the product first, or build the system first? I'd actually written about this exact tension in Design System — Do we really need?, and the short answer I landed on is that a system earns its place once the chaos it's meant to organize actually exists — not before.

For this platform, that moment arrived fast. The product was a white-label fintech platform for corporate and retail banking clients across different regions, and every client needed the same core experience, adapted to their own market. That's a lot of moving parts to keep consistent by hand.

The problem

Build a scalable design system from scratch for this platform. It had to line up design and development, adapt to regional needs, hold up under high-volume, high-stakes financial workflows, and support more than one product launching at once.

It's really just about organizing your design chaos so your future self — and your team — will thank you. From Design System — Do we really need?

That became the whole premise of the work that followed.

My process

How do you define tokens, fields, components, all the atoms and molecules — without knowing what the product looks like? But then again, how do you build consistent screens without a system to pull from? From How do you define tokens, fields, components…

So I didn't start by defining anything permanent. I started with rough wireframes — grey boxes, placeholders, general layout zones — just enough to know where things would live, not enough to systematize yet.

From there, real screens got tested in real contexts before any token or component was locked down: could users read the hierarchy, was it accessible, did it hold up responsively. That sandbox phase was messy by design, and it's what told me which components the product actually needed, rather than which ones looked good in isolation. Only once the system had, as I called it then, "a pulse," did I start treating it like a living product — versioning components, documenting just enough to keep designers and developers self-sufficient, and evolving the logic as new requirements came in.

  1. Audited what existed. I reviewed every UI element, from buttons and colors to cards and dropdowns, and listed the duplicates and inconsistencies.
  2. Set principles. Accessibility, consistency and flexibility became the compass for every later decision.
  3. Built the visual style guide. Type scales, color tokens, iconography and spacing rules, documented once.
  4. Created a reusable component library. Buttons, forms, navigation and modals, each with usage guidance and edge cases.
  5. Wrote everything down. The what, why and how behind each component, so designers and developers could self-serve.
  6. Added version control. I tracked changes with Git and token management so updates would not break things.
  7. Brought people along. I ran demos, onboarded teams and answered questions to show how the system made work faster and more consistent.

Looking ahead

Ten months on from when I wrote that, I'd build this differently. I'd lean on component-level tokens from the very start, so one system could flex across multiple brands, not just multiple regions. It's a natural next step from the scoped-token work already in this system — global tokens for the shared core, local tokens for what each brand needs to make its own.

What went wrong, and what I learned

Every designer dreams of crafting the perfect design system: a golden source of truth, a beacon of consistency, a button that doesn't multiply like rabbits. I was that designer — ambitious, excited, and very, very wrong. From How I broke my own design system (so you don't have to)

The first mistake was the classic one: I started building the system before there was a product worth systematizing. No screens tested, no real constraints yet — just a designer with Figma dreams and a love of consistency, building structure for structure's sake.

The second was more specific, and more painful. In an effort to keep things DRY, I built a single mega-component called "input field" that tried to handle every situation at once. It didn't work. Every new use case needed its own override, until it became, in my own words, "the most duplicated and least loved item in the system" — the opposite of what a reusable component is supposed to be.

I also didn't set up version control early enough. Tracking changes became difficult, designers started detaching instances to work around the system instead of with it, and rolling out updates got chaotic fast. And there was a real tension I had to keep managing between structure and freedom — too much rigidity risked killing the creativity the system was supposed to support, not replace.

The trick? Make the rules feel like freedom. From Constructing a Design System for Designers

The goal was never a brand prison — rules strict enough to prevent chaos, but breathable enough that nobody felt like they were designing inside a locked-down government portal. The lesson, in the end, was simple to say and hard to live: put the structure in place before scale arrives, not after.

How I measured it

  • Adoption: how many teams started using the system
  • Component reuse: more reuse meant less time wasted on one-off designs
  • Consistency: fewer UI mismatches across teams and screens over time
  • Development speed: time saved by ready-to-use components
  • Feedback: fewer complaints and smoother experiences

The thinking behind this system didn't stay contained to this one project, either. The same instinct for structure later shaped an internal platform at Infosys that helps manage retirement funds for approximately 282,600 employees across India — a completely separate build, with nothing carried over directly, but the same underlying approach to turning chaos into something people can actually rely on.
